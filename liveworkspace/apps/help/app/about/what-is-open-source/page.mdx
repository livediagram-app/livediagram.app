Most software you use is a black box: you get the finished app, but the source code that makes it work is kept private. **Open source** flips that around. The code is published in the open for anyone to read, run, modify, and share. <AppName /> is open source, and that shapes the whole product, from what it costs to how your data is handled.

## What "Open Source" Actually Means

Software is open source when its source code, the human-readable instructions that make the program work, is published under a licence that grants everyone the right to use it freely. In practice that means four things:

- **You can read it.** Nothing about how the app works is hidden. The exact code that runs is the code you can inspect.
- **You can use it.** Anyone may use the code, for any purpose.
- **You can modify it.** Fork it, change it, adapt it to your needs.
- **You can share it.** Pass on the original or your modified version.

The licence is what makes those rights real rather than a promise. Different open-source licences attach different conditions; <AppName /> uses one of the most permissive.

## The MIT Licence

<AppName /> is released under the **MIT licence**, one of the simplest and most permissive open-source licences. It is permissive on purpose: anyone may read, use, fork, modify, or embed the code, for any purpose, commercial or not, with no copyleft strings attached. The whole codebase is publicly viewable.

That licence choice has concrete consequences for you.

### It Is Free, With No Paid Tier

Because the project is open and there is no commercial product to protect, <AppName /> is **free for everyone**. There is no paid tier, no "Pro" upgrade, and no plan to introduce one. Every feature ships to every user.
